## Grounding snapshot

**Origin.** Split by playmaker on 260814-1733 out of the user's hand-written observation dump, and promoted here on 260906 from `260814-1733_*_bounded-executor-dispatches.md`. The filed wording carried two halves. The bounded-dispatch half is adopted; the re-injection half is refuted by `260812-0303-simplify-speed-and-why-rules-do-not-hold.md`, which found that the rules never held rather than that they decayed, and the user confirmed the narrowing when this Circle was drafted.

**What the cost claim rests on, corrected.** The source analysis states one 200-call dispatch as re-sending 15.9M tokens of accumulated tool output against 3.9M for four 50-call dispatches, and prints the ratio as roughly fourfold. The arithmetic reconstructs exactly from the one parameter the same analysis gives elsewhere, 800 tokens of output per tool call, summed quadratically over the run. What follows is that splitting a run of N calls into k dispatches divides the re-sent volume by approximately k, so **the number four is the split count that example chose and not a measured saving**. Eight dispatches of 25 calls would have printed eight. Two of the inputs are asserted rather than sourced: the 800 tokens per call carries no measurement anywhere, and the 200-call run length is an illustration.

**The premise that turned volume into cost is false as stated, and there is a counter-effect.** The record previously carried the sentence "accumulated tool output is not cacheable". It is refuted against the API documentation: `cache_control` sits on `tool_result` blocks, the documented multi-turn pattern reuses the whole accumulated prefix, and a cache read costs about a tenth of plain input price. The counter-effect runs against the remedy rather than merely weakening it. Four dispatches are four fresh contexts: each writes its own prefix at about 1.25 times input price, none can read the previous dispatch's accumulated tail because that tail is not in its prompt, and the cache entry expires five minutes after the request that wrote or last read it began. **A split can lower re-sent volume and raise the bill at the same time.** The size and even the sign of the net effect cannot be established from anything in this tree, and that residual is filed as `260907-0820_*_is-a-token-side-measurement-of-the-splits-net-cost-worth-building.md`.

**Where the five-minute lifetime bites, and where it does not.** A cache read refreshes the timer at no cost, so inside one long dispatch the entry stays warm however long the dispatch runs. The expiry applies across the gap between dispatches and not within one, which is exactly where the split argument uses it. Measured on 260907 over the 97 machine-written handoff gaps in this workbench's event log that are shorter than 24 hours: the median gap is **2.37 minutes**, **38 of them, 39.2 percent, exceed five minutes**, and those 38 carry 97.7 percent of all handoff minutes. So the claim that five minutes is shorter than many orchestrator round trips holds, on a distribution whose middle sits well inside the lifetime and whose tail carries almost all of the elapsed time. That is the whole of what durations can settle about the cache question, and it is why the spec's second stopping condition was withdrawn rather than restated.

**The free handoff is measured in minutes, and the argument is denominated in tokens.** The source measures `dispatch → dispatch` at 0.0 minutes at the median across both projects it read, and 1.28 hours in total over 60 fusion transitions. That is wall clock between two event rows. A handoff taking no measurable wall clock still costs a fresh prefix, a report the orchestrator reads, and a re-dispatch prompt that restates the state, all of which are tokens. The user accepted the minute measurement as sufficient evidence on 260907, knowing it is not in the currency of the argument, and required the limitation to be written down rather than left implicit. It is written down here and in the spec's C5.

**What separates a rule that holds from one that does not, with the figure corrected.** The source contrasts an obligation that rides an act the agent performs anyway with one that stands alone. Measured over the 1265 event rows written before 2026-08-12, the orchestrator emitted 177 `task_start` rows against 248 `task_done` rows, which is **at least 28.6 percent** dropped. The figure is a floor rather than a point estimate: its denominator is the count of announced completions, so a dispatch that announced neither row is invisible to it and the true rate can only be higher. Two wrong figures stood here before it. The record first carried 36 percent, which reconstructs from no input the source states. It then carried 30.6 percent beside the 28.6, from pairing the rows by their own `task` field; that one is withdrawn on 260907 because it reproduces under none of four pairing methods over the same window, which give 28.6, 30.2, 34.7 and 37.1 percent. The 28.6 percent is method-free and carries the argument on its own. The companion claim of "248 of 248" for the attached obligation is gone: its denominator was the `task_done` count itself, so it could not have been a compliance rate. The contrast stands on the source's other two instances, the session bookkeeping that froze after one Turn and the rule broken three commits after it landed.

**What the event log can and cannot measure.** Verified over all rows of `orchestrator-events.jsonl` in this workbench: the fields present are `ts`, `event`, `detail`, `agent`, `turn`, `task`, `verdict`, `history_file`, `person`, `checkout`, `session_id`, `artifact_grounding`, `artifact_directive`, `grounding_directive`, `clause`. No token field exists, and no event records a tool call inside a dispatch. Duration is therefore the only quantity the log yields per dispatch, which is why the user rejected a before-and-after measurement as the closure standard.

**The hook measurement, taken live on 260907 and no longer open.** The PreToolUse hook does fire for a tool call made inside a sub-agent's run, and the `guard_allow` row it writes carries the parent orchestrator's `session_id`, measured at 05:00:39Z during the planability check. Three limits come with that answer. Only write tools produce a row: `Read`, `Grep` and `Glob` reach no hook at all, and `Bash` reaches it and writes nothing by design. The row carries no agent identity. And a hook can deny a tool call but cannot make a sub-agent return with a handoff, while `SubagentStop` fires after the run has already ended.

**What that settles about the mechanism.** *Has the agent reached its bound* is decidable for wall clock, from the `task_start` row the machine already writes — but only inside an orchestrator session. `hooks/lib/orchestrator-events.ts` gates every machine-written row on Setup's state file, so a dispatch made by a skill body or by a user running an agent directly produces no row, has no orchestrator to compute a stopping time for it, and has no continuation mechanism behind it. Those dispatches run unbounded, and the reading afterwards cannot see them; the spec states both rather than implying coverage it does not have. *Can the agent be made to return* is decidable by nothing fusion has, because the only cooperative surface is the agent's own prompt. This Circle therefore buys a **requested** bound plus a reading afterwards of whether the request was honoured, and the spec states that plainly along with what it costs the expectation of saving.

**The reserved questions are answered, and one answer was narrowed the same day.** The bound is measured in wall-clock time, settled by the user on 260907 and unchanged since. Its value is **20 minutes**, settled by the user on 260907 in the second round, replacing a property ("at or above the median of the recorded durations") that left a half-open interval and a median that moved between 9.35 and 11.93 minutes with the population read.

Who the bound covers was answered twice. The first answer, on 260907, was **all dispatched agents**. It was **narrowed the same day, by the user, to the agents whose work lands on disk as they go**, after the second planability check found that for an agent whose product exists only at the end, the return report would have to be the state rather than a pointer to it. The superseded answer is recorded here rather than overwritten, so that a later reader meets the narrowing instead of a set they cannot account for.

**The seven agents the bound covers**, each checked at its own prompt on 260907: `coder` and `ontocoder` edit source and data files as they work; `bugfixer` applies its fix to the tree; `reconciler` updates each tracking file in turn; `coderev` and `ontorev` write one working file per topic through the run; `curator` in its apply pass writes each approved entry and records its outcome per entry. **The seven exempt agents:** `taskplanner`, whose product is its report and never a file; `analyst`, `consultant`, `shaper`, `planner` and `editor`, each producing one document written at the end; and `playmaker`, which overwrites the portfolio whole. Two of these differ from the list the second check proposed, and both were checked at the prompt rather than adopted: `planner` is exempt because its plan is step 5 of its own process and nothing of it exists on disk before then, and `curator` is bound because its apply pass is the pass that does the work.

**What the bound reaches, measured on 260907** over the 131 machine-written dispatch pairs in this workbench's event log: 15 pairs, 11.5 percent, ran past 20 minutes. Over the 114 of those made by an agent the bound now covers, 13, 11.4 percent, did. Only four of the seven exempt agents appear in that log at all, so the exemption removes 17 pairs and 2 of the 15 long dispatches from the bound's reach.
